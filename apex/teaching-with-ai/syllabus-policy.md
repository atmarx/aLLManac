# Your syllabus policy

## Why the single course-wide rule fails

"No AI in this course" is unenforceable, and after [integrity and detection](integrity-and-detection.md) it is also indefensible to enforce — you would be acting on instruments that misfire hardest against your non-native English speakers.

"AI is allowed" tells a student nothing about the assignment in front of them.

Both fail the same way.  The right answer legitimately differs per assignment, because the capability being assessed differs per assignment.  A course-wide rule has to be either so strict it is fiction or so loose it is silent.

So split it: **the syllabus carries the principle, and each assignment carries the rule.**

## The principle, which is one sentence

> You are fully responsible for everything you submit.  A tool cannot be accountable for your work; only you can.

Almost everything else follows from that, and it has the considerable advantage of being true regardless of what models can do next year.  It also sidesteps the unanswerable question — *did you use AI* — in favor of the one that has always mattered.

## What belongs in the syllabus

Five things, briefly:

1. **The principle above**, in your own words.
2. **That the rule varies by assignment**, and where students will find it.
3. **The disclosure expectation** when use is permitted.
4. **What happens if you have concerns** — specifically that a detector score is not evidence and the first step is a conversation.  Students who know this are meaningfully less afraid of you, and fear produces concealment.
5. **Who to ask when the rule is unclear**, with the default being *ask*.

## What belongs on the assignment

A named permission level, on the assignment sheet itself rather than only in the syllabus.  Four levels cover nearly everything, and a small reusable set is far easier for students to follow than prose written fresh each time.

| Level | Short form |
|---|---|
| **1 — None** | No AI assistance on this one, and here is why |
| **2 — Specified steps** | Permitted for named parts, not for others |
| **3 — Open, disclosed** | Use what helps; tell me what you used |
| **4 — Required, assessed** | Using it is the assignment; your judgment is graded |

## Drop-in language

Adapt freely.  These are written in second person because that is how students read a syllabus.

### The syllabus paragraph

> **AI tools in this course.**  You are fully responsible for everything you submit.  A tool cannot be accountable for your work — only you can.  That means every claim in your work is yours to support, whatever helped you produce it, and "the AI said so" is not an explanation I can accept any more than a journal editor or an employer could.
>
> Whether AI assistance is permitted depends on the assignment, because different assignments are assessing different things.  Each assignment sheet states its level.  When it is not clear, ask me — asking is never held against you.
>
> If I have a concern about a submission, I will talk to you about it.  I do not use AI detectors; they are unreliable and they misfire hardest against students who write English as a second language.  What I will ask you to do is walk me through your sources and your process, which is a normal thing to ask of any scholar.

### Level 1 — No AI assistance

> **AI use: none.**  Do not use AI assistance for this assignment.  It is assessing [*name the capability*], and getting help with that specific thing removes the point of doing it.  You will do this kind of work again under conditions where no tool is available, and this is the practice.
>
> Tools you already use for accessibility or basic spelling and grammar are fine and always have been.

### Level 2 — Specified steps only

> **AI use: permitted for specific steps.**  You may use AI assistance to [*e.g. brainstorm topics, generate practice problems, translate technical vocabulary, debug syntax errors*].  Do not use it to [*e.g. draft your analysis, write your conclusions, summarize sources you have not read*].
>
> The line is where the assignment starts assessing you rather than preparing you.  Note at the end of your submission which of the permitted uses you made.

### Level 3 — Open, with disclosure

> **AI use: open, with disclosure.**  Use whatever helps you produce good work.  At the end of your submission, add one or two lines: what tool, what you used it for, and what you changed about the output.
>
> Disclosure is documentation, not confession — it is not held against you and it does not lower your grade.  What is assessed is the work itself, and every claim in it is yours to defend.  Sources will be checked, so verify that anything you cite exists and says what you say it says.

### Level 4 — Required, and the use is assessed

> **AI use: required.**  This assignment is about working with the tool, so use it and show your work with it.  Submit your prompts, the output you received, and your critique of that output — what it got right, what it got wrong, what it assumed, and what you changed.
>
> You are graded on your judgment about the output rather than on the output.  A submission that accepts a flawed answer uncritically scores below one that catches the flaw, whatever the final text looks like.

## Disclosure that people actually use

One line at the end of a submission: **what tool, what for, what you changed.**

Long reflective statements sound more rigorous and get written more carelessly.  Give students a template and one filled-in example, and you will get usable disclosure instead of a paragraph of throat-clearing.

Say plainly that disclosure is not an admission of wrongdoing.  If students suspect it counts against them, honest students under-report and you have taught the wrong lesson to exactly the wrong people.

## What to say about verification

Whatever the level, the standard for claims does not move.  Worth a line in the syllabus and a reminder on assignments where sources matter:

> Any source you cite must exist and must say what you claim it says.  I check some of them.  This is not about AI — it is the standard for citing anything — but AI-assisted work fails it more often, so verify before you submit.

[Verifying sources](verifying-sources.md) covers the checking side, including what to do about it in your own grading.

## Aligning with your institution

Your institution's academic integrity policy is the authority, and a course policy **narrows** it rather than overriding it.  Find yours before the term starts, and check two things specifically: whether it mandates detector use (if so, treat output as a flag for human review and document the review), and what the process is when you have a concern.

If your institution has adopted language you must reproduce, use it, and add the per-assignment levels underneath it.  The two layers do not conflict.
