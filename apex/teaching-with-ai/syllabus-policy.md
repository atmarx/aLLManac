## Why the single course-wide rule fails

<!-- "No AI in this course" is unenforceable and, after
     integrity-and-detection.md, indefensible to enforce.  "AI allowed" tells
     a student nothing about the assignment in front of them.  Both fail for
     the same reason: the answer legitimately differs per assignment, because
     the capability being assessed differs per assignment.

     So the syllabus carries the FRAME, and each assignment carries the
     RULE. -->

## What belongs in the syllabus

<!-- - Your stance, in a sentence a student can repeat
     - That the rule varies by assignment, and where to find it
     - The disclosure expectation when use is permitted
     - What happens if you suspect misuse — specifically, that a detector
       score is not evidence and that the first step is a conversation
     - Who to ask when the rule is unclear, with the default being "ask" -->

## What belongs on the assignment

<!-- The per-assignment line, in student-actionable language.  A small set of
     reusable levels is easier to follow than prose written fresh each time:

     - **No AI assistance.**  Say why, briefly — students follow rules they
       understand.
     - **AI for specified steps only** (brainstorming, outlining, debugging,
       translating), with the rest your own.
     - **AI allowed, disclosed.**  Say what to disclose.
     - **AI required**, with the use itself assessed.

     Name the level on the assignment sheet, not only in the syllabus. -->

## Disclosure that is actually usable

<!-- One line at the end of the submission: what tool, what for, what you
