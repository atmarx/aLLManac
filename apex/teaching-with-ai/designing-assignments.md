# Designing assignments

<!-- SCAFFOLD. -->

## The question that comes first

<!-- Name the capability the assignment is meant to observe.  Not the
     deliverable — the capability.  "A five-page analysis" is a deliverable;
     "can they construct an argument from conflicting sources" is what you are
     assessing.  Most AI panic dissolves or sharpens once that sentence
     exists, because you can then ask whether the model does that thing. -->

## Three honest categories

<!-- Work through the sort, with examples from real course shapes:

     1. **AI short-circuits it.**  The capability IS the thing the model
        does.  Summarizing a reading to show you read it; producing prose to
        show you can write prose.  These need to change or move.
     2. **AI scaffolds it.**  The model removes friction that was never the
        point — boilerplate code, formatting, a first draft to react against,
        translating jargon.  Often these get BETTER with AI allowed, and
        forbidding it mostly penalizes students who need the scaffold most.
     3. **AI barely touches it.**  Lab work, studio critique, fieldwork,
        anything grounded in this week's specific context, anything assessed
        in conversation.  Leave these alone. -->

## When an assignment lands in category one

<!-- Options, in rough order of effort, none of them "ban it and hope":
     - move the assessment to conditions you control
     - change what is submitted (process over product — see
       integrity-and-detection.md)
     - raise the floor: if the model can do the old task, assess the thing
       one level up — critique, selection, judgment about the output
     - make the AI use explicit and grade the judgment
     - decide the capability no longer needs assessing, which is sometimes
       the right answer and deserves saying out loud -->

## The trap worth naming

<!-- Redesigning every assignment at once, badly, in August.  Pick the two or
     three where the mismatch is worst.  A course with three well-adapted
     assignments and eight unchanged ones is in better shape than one with
     eleven hasty rewrites. -->

## Equity considerations that are easy to miss

<!-- - Students arrive with very different AI fluency, and "just use AI" is
       as unequal as "never use AI" if nobody is taught how.
     - Requiring a paid tool disadvantages students; this platform exists
       partly so the course tool is the institution's rather than a
       subscription.
     - Process-visibility requirements (drafts, logs) cost time, which is not
       evenly available to students working jobs or carrying care duties.
       Worth designing so the trail is a byproduct rather than extra labor. -->
