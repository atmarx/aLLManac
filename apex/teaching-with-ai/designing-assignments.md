# Designing assignments

## The question that comes first

Before writing any rule about AI, name **the capability the assignment is meant to observe.**  Not the deliverable — the capability.

"A five-page analysis of two conflicting sources" is a deliverable.  "Can they recognize that two sources disagree, and construct a defensible position anyway" is the capability.  Most of the difficulty around AI dissolves or sharpens the moment that second sentence exists, because you can then ask a concrete question: *does the model do that thing?*

If you cannot write the capability sentence for an assignment, that is worth knowing on its own, and it is a problem that predates the technology.

## Three honest categories

Sort each assignment.  All three appear in most courses, often in the same week.

### 1.  AI short-circuits it

The capability you are assessing **is** the thing the model does.

Summarizing a reading to demonstrate you read it.  Producing competent prose to demonstrate you can produce competent prose.  Generating a standard literature overview.  Writing a first-year essay whose point is the writing.

These need to change or move.  A rule alone will not save them, because the rule has to be enforced with instruments that do not work.

### 2.  AI scaffolds it

The model removes friction that was never what you were assessing.

Boilerplate code so the student gets to the algorithm.  Formatting a bibliography.  Translating dense jargon so a non-specialist can engage with a paper.  Producing a bad first draft to react against, which is a real technique and always has been.

These often get **better** with AI permitted, and forbidding it mostly penalizes the students who needed the scaffold most — the ones for whom the blank page or the unfamiliar register was the actual barrier rather than the thinking.

### 3.  AI barely touches it

Lab and studio work.  Fieldwork.  Anything grounded in the specific context of this room, this dataset, this patient, this week's discussion.  Anything assessed in conversation.

Leave these alone.  A surprising number of assignments are already here, and faculty rewrite them out of a general anxiety that does not apply.

## When an assignment lands in category one

Options, roughly in order of effort, none of which are "ban it and hope."

**Move the assessment into conditions you control.**  In-class writing, an oral component, a five-minute conversation about the submission.  Expensive per student, decisive where the stakes justify it.

**Change what gets submitted.**  Ask for the process rather than only the product — outline, annotated sources, drafts, the revision history.  A finished artifact is easy to generate; a coherent trail of thinking behind it is considerably harder and more useful to grade.

**Raise the floor.**  This is the move worth reaching for first.  If the model can now do the old task, assess the level above it: not *summarize this paper*, but *evaluate this summary against the paper and identify what it distorts*.  Not *write a function that does X*, but *here are three implementations, choose one for this context and defend the choice*.

The capability you were assessing has not disappeared.  It has become table stakes, and the interesting question moved up a level.  Assignments rebuilt this way tend to be better than what they replaced, which is the only genuinely good news in this whole subject.

**Make the AI use the assignment.**  Require the prompts, the raw output, and the student's critique of it.  This converts an integrity problem into a skills assessment, and their judgment about the output is a better read on their understanding than the essay was.

**Decide the capability no longer needs assessing.**  Sometimes the honest answer, and it deserves to be said out loud rather than arrived at by neglect.  Be deliberate: some capabilities that look obsolete are load-bearing for the ones above them.

## The trap

Redesigning every assignment at once, badly, in August.

Pick the two or three where the mismatch is worst.  A course with three well-adapted assignments and eight unchanged ones is in far better shape than one carrying eleven hasty rewrites, and you will learn more from doing a few carefully than from converting the whole syllabus on instinct.

## Equity, in the parts that get missed

**Fluency is unevenly distributed.**  "Just use AI" is as unequal a starting line as "never use AI" when nobody is taught how.  Some students arrive having used these tools for two years; others have never opened one and will not admit it.

**Requiring a paid tool disadvantages students.**  Part of why a course platform exists at all is so the tool is the institution's rather than a personal subscription — everyone gets the same model and the same budget.

**Process-visibility requirements cost time**, and time is not evenly available to students working jobs or carrying care responsibilities.  Design so the trail is a byproduct of the work rather than a second assignment stapled to the first — a shared document with revision history costs nothing; a reflective process journal costs hours.

**Verification requirements are the fair kind of demand.**  Checking that your sources say what you claim is work every student can do, it does not depend on having good instincts about a tool, and it is the same standard for everyone.

## Two worked examples

### A research paper in a mid-level seminar

*Capability:* can they locate relevant scholarship, read it accurately, and build an argument that accounts for what it says.

*Category:* one.  A model produces a competent version of this deliverable.

*What changed:* the paper stays, and three things get added.  Sources must be submitted as a verified list — each with a resolving link and one sentence on what it specifically supports.  Two claims of the instructor's choosing must be traceable to a quoted passage.  The final section asks the student to name the strongest objection to their own argument, which is the part models do blandly and students do well once asked.

*What it cost:* about ten extra minutes of grading per paper, concentrated on the source list, where problems surface fastest.

### An introductory programming assignment

*Capability:* can they decompose a problem into steps a machine can execute.

*Category:* looks like one, is mostly two.  The model writes the syntax instantly, and syntax was never the capability — decomposition was.

*What changed:* the code is no longer the submission on its own.  Students submit the decomposition first, in plain language, before writing anything.  The code follows, from whatever source.  The grade weights the decomposition and a short defense of one design choice.

*What it cost:* less than the old version, because the plain-language decomposition is faster to grade than debugging everyone's syntax — and it assesses the thing the course was always about.

## Where this connects

Once assignments are sorted, the rule per assignment is short and easy to write.  That is [your syllabus policy](syllabus-policy.md), and it is the easier half of this work — which is why it comes second.
