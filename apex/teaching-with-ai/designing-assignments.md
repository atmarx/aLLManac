# Designing assignments

## The question that comes first

Before writing any rule about AI, name **the capability the assignment is meant to observe.**  The capability is different from the deliverable, which is the thing the student hands in.

"A five-page analysis of two conflicting sources" is a deliverable.  "Can they recognize that two sources disagree, and construct a defensible position anyway" is the capability.  Once the capability sentence is written, most of the difficulty around AI either goes away or becomes specific.  You can then ask a concrete question: *does the model do that thing?*

If you cannot write the capability sentence for an assignment, the assignment had a problem before AI arrived.

## Three categories

Sort each assignment into one of three categories.  All three appear in most courses, often in the same week.

### 1.  AI short-circuits it

The capability you are assessing **is** the thing the model does.

Summarizing a reading to demonstrate you read it.  Producing competent prose to demonstrate you can produce competent prose.  Generating a standard literature overview.  Writing a first-year essay whose point is the writing.

Assignments in this category need to change or move.  A rule alone will not protect them.  The rule would have to be enforced with instruments that do not work.

### 2.  AI scaffolds it

The model removes friction that was never what you were assessing.

Boilerplate code so the student gets to the algorithm.  Formatting a bibliography.  Translating dense jargon so a non-specialist can engage with a paper.  Producing a bad first draft to react against, a technique writers have always used.

Assignments in this category often get **better** with AI permitted.  Forbidding AI mostly penalizes the students who needed the scaffold most: the ones whose barrier was the blank page or the unfamiliar register, and not the thinking.

### 3.  AI barely touches it

Lab and studio work.  Fieldwork.  Anything grounded in the specific context of this room, this dataset, this patient, this week's discussion.  Anything assessed in conversation.

Leave assignments in this category alone.  A surprising number of assignments already belong here, and faculty rewrite them out of a general anxiety that does not apply to them.

## When an assignment is in category one

A category-one assignment has five options, listed roughly in order of effort.  None of them is to ban AI and hope.

**Move the assessment into conditions you control.**  Examples are in-class writing, an oral component, or a five-minute conversation about the submission.  This option is expensive per student, and it settles the question where the stakes justify the cost.

**Change what gets submitted.**  Ask for the process as well as the product: the outline, annotated sources, drafts, and the revision history.  A finished artifact is easy to generate.  A coherent trail of thinking behind it is considerably harder to generate and more useful to grade.

**Raise the floor.**  Try raising the floor first.  If the model can now do the old task, assess the level above it.  Instead of *summarize this paper*, ask students to *evaluate this summary against the paper and identify what it distorts*.  Instead of *write a function that does X*, give them *three implementations, and ask them to choose one for this context and defend the choice*.

When you raise the floor, the capability you were assessing has not disappeared.  It has become a baseline, and the more interesting question is one level up.  Assignments rebuilt this way tend to be better than the ones they replaced.

**Make the AI use the assignment.**  Require the prompts, the raw output, and the student's critique of it.  Making AI use the assignment turns an integrity problem into a skills assessment.  The student's judgment about the output shows their understanding better than the essay did.

**Decide the capability no longer needs assessing.**  Sometimes dropping a capability is the right answer.  If it is, decide it openly and say so, instead of letting it happen by neglect.  Choose carefully.  Some capabilities that look obsolete are the foundation for the ones above them.

## The trap

The common mistake is to redesign every assignment at once in August, and to do it badly.

Instead, pick the two or three assignments where the mismatch is worst.  A course with three well-adapted assignments and eight unchanged ones is in far better shape than one with eleven hasty rewrites, and you will learn more from doing a few carefully than from converting the whole syllabus on instinct.

## Equity, in the parts that get missed

Fluency with AI tools is unevenly distributed.  "Just use AI" is as unequal a starting line as "never use AI" when nobody is taught how.  Some students arrive having used these tools for years.  Others have never opened one and will not admit it.

Requiring a paid tool disadvantages students.  A course platform exists partly so the tool belongs to the institution and nobody needs a personal subscription.  Everyone gets the same model and draws on the same course budget.

**Process-visibility requirements cost time**, and time is not evenly available to students working jobs or with care responsibilities.  Design the requirement so the trail of work is a byproduct of doing the work, with no second assignment stapled to the first.  A shared document with revision history costs the student nothing.  A reflective process journal costs hours.

Verification requirements are the fair kind of demand.  Checking that your sources say what you claim is work every student can do.  It does not depend on having good instincts about a tool, and the standard is the same for everyone.

## Two worked examples

### A research paper in a mid-level seminar

*Capability:* can they locate relevant scholarship, read it accurately, and build an argument that accounts for what it says.

*Category:* one.  A model produces a competent version of this deliverable.

*What changed:* the paper stays, and three things get added.  Sources must be submitted as a verified list, each with a resolving link and one sentence on what it specifically supports.  Two claims of the instructor's choosing must be traceable to a quoted passage.  The final section asks the student to name the strongest objection to their own argument.  Models do that part blandly, and students do it well once asked.

*What it cost:* about ten extra minutes of grading per paper, concentrated on the source list, where problems surface fastest.

### An introductory programming assignment

*Capability:* can they decompose a problem into steps a machine can execute.

*Category:* it looks like category one, but it is mostly category two.  The model writes the syntax instantly.  The capability being assessed is decomposition, and syntax was never part of it.

*What changed:* the code is no longer the submission on its own.  Students submit the decomposition first, in plain language, before writing anything.  The code follows, from whatever source.  The grade weights the decomposition and a short defense of one design choice.

*What it cost:* less than the old version.  A plain-language decomposition is faster to grade than debugging everyone's syntax, and it assesses the thing the course was always about.
