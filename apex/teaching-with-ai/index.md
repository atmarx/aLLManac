# Teaching with AI

Students in your course have access to a capable language model through an environment with your course's name on it.  That access changes some assignments and leaves others alone, and working out which is which is a teaching question.

You already know how to design your own course.  What is usually missing is specific information about a tool that arrived faster than the guidance for it.

## The principle underneath all of it

**A student is fully responsible for what they submit.  A model cannot accept blame; only a person can.**

Once a student's responsibility is settled, most of the hard questions get easier.  The question changes from *did you use AI* to *do you stand behind this*.  The first question cannot be answered, and it matters less every year.  The second is the one scholarship has always asked.

The accountability principle also avoids a double standard.  Faculty and staff use these tools every day to write and to automate.  Expecting students not to is a fiction that teaches them to conceal their process.  The workable expectation is the one professionals already follow: use what you like, and answer for the result.

### And the reason that is hard

Answering for a piece of text means having read it.  That is difficult with this tool, and the tool's name explains why.  **Large language models produce large amounts of language.**  A model writes a page in seconds, and reading a page properly takes minutes.  Output piles up faster than anyone can read it, so it piles up faster than anyone can be accountable for it.

The gap between how fast a model writes and how fast a person reads is arithmetic, so telling students to "be more careful" does not fix it.  [AI literacy](ai-literacy.md) covers the problem in detail.  Its most underrated fix is to generate less.

## Start with the design question

The order that causes trouble is policy first and design second: writing a rule about AI before deciding what the assignment assesses.  A rule written that way has to do a job it can't do, and it gets enforced with tools that do not work.

The order that works for planning an assignment:

1. **What is this assignment assessing?**  Name the capability you are trying to observe.
2. **Does AI assistance short-circuit that, scaffold it, or leave it untouched?**  All three happen, often within the same course.
3. **Then** write the rule, per assignment, in language a student can act on.

[Designing assignments](designing-assignments.md) works through these three steps in detail.

## Before the term starts

If your plan for academic integrity depends on an AI detector, read [Integrity and detection](integrity-and-detection.md) before the term starts.  Detectors do not work reliably, and their failures fall hardest on non-native English writers.  Some institutions have switched detectors off.  [Vanderbilt](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/) did in 2023.

The practice that replaced detectors is [verifying sources](verifying-sources.md): checking that cited work exists and says what the citation claims.  Source verification is the accountability principle in a form you can put on an assignment.
