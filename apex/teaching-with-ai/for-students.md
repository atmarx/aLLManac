# Using AI well in your coursework

<!-- SCAFFOLD.

     TONE: talk to students like adults making decisions about their own
     learning.  No scolding, no lecture about cheating, no breathless
     enthusiasm.  The honest frame is that this tool can genuinely help and
     can genuinely cost them the thing they came here for, and which one
     depends on choices they control.

     This page also does the overhearing work in reverse — faculty read what
     their students are told and calibrate their own expectations. -->

## The trade nobody explains

<!-- The useful opening: struggling with something is not a sign the tool
     should take over.  Difficulty is frequently where the learning is
     happening, and the model is very good at removing exactly that
     difficulty.

     But the reverse is also true — friction that is NOT the point (jargon,
     formatting, a blank page) is worth removing, and refusing help there is
     not virtue.

     Give them the question to ask themselves: *what am I supposed to be
     getting better at here, and does this help me get better at it or skip
     it?* -->

## Check what it tells you

<!-- Concrete failure modes, in student language:
     - citations that look perfect and do not exist
     - confident answers about anything recent, local, or niche
     - reasoning that is right until one step in the middle
     - going along with a wrong assumption you put in the question

     The rule: the more confident and fluent it sounds, the less that tells
     you.  Fluency is not evidence. -->

## Know your course's rules, and ask when unclear

<!-- Rules legitimately differ per assignment because assignments assess
     different things.  Asking is not an admission of anything.

     Also worth telling them: if a rule seems arbitrary, ask what the
     assignment is assessing.  Usually the answer makes the rule obvious. -->

## Say what you used

<!-- Disclosure, framed as normal professional practice rather than
     confession.  One line: what tool, what for, what you changed.  Note that
     disclosing is not admitting to wrongdoing — under a permissive policy it
     is just documentation. -->

## If you are accused because of a detector

<!-- Students deserve to know this, and it is the reason the page carries
     weight.

     AI detectors are unreliable, and they are unreliable unevenly: research
     testing seven detectors found essays by non-native English writers
     flagged as AI-generated most of the time, while native writers' essays
     were almost never misflagged.  The detectors key on simple, predictable
     phrasing, which describes good second-language writing.

     So: a detector score is not evidence.  Ask for the specific concern, ask
     what else it is based on, and offer to talk through your drafts and your
     process.  Keeping your outlines and drafts is worth doing for this
     reason alone.

     Link integrity-and-detection.md — students should be able to see what
     their instructors were told. -->
